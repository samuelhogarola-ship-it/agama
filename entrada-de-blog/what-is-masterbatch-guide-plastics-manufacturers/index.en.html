What Is Masterbatch? A Complete Guide for Plastics Manufacturers

Masterbatch is one of the most fundamental materials in plastics processing, yet it is often misunderstood by buyers entering the industry. This guide covers everything from the basic definition to practical advice on choosing the right masterbatch for your application.

Masterbatch defined

Masterbatch is a concentrated mixture of pigments, dyes, or additives encapsulated in a carrier resin. It is manufactured as solid pellets or granules and is designed to be blended with a base (natural) polymer resin during plastics processing. The result is a uniformly colored or functionally enhanced plastic part.

Think of it as a concentrated seasoning for plastic. Instead of adding raw pigment powder directly to the resin, which creates dispersion problems and dust hazards, you add pre-dispersed, pre-measured pellets that melt and distribute evenly throughout the polymer matrix during processing.

How masterbatch is made

The manufacturing process involves three core components and a compounding step:

1. Carrier resin

The carrier resin is the polymer base that holds the pigment or additive concentrate. It must be compatible with the base resin the processor will use. Common carrier resins include low-density polyethylene (LDPE) for polyolefin applications, polystyrene (PS) for styrenics, and universal carriers designed for broad compatibility.

The carrier resin typically makes up 40% to 70% of the masterbatch by weight, depending on the pigment loading and the target let-down ratio.

2. Pigment or additive concentrate

This is the functional ingredient. For color masterbatch, it is one or more pigments selected for the target color, lightfastness, heat stability, and chemical resistance required by the application. For additive masterbatch, it is a functional additive like a UV stabilizer, antioxidant, slip agent, or anti-static compound.

Pigment loading in color masterbatch typically ranges from 15% to 65% by weight. Higher loadings allow lower let-down ratios, which means the processor uses less masterbatch per kilogram of natural resin.

3. Dispersing agents and processing aids

Waxes, stearates, and other dispersing agents help the pigment particles separate uniformly within the carrier resin during compounding. These are used in small percentages but are critical to achieving consistent color and preventing agglomeration.

4. Compounding

The carrier resin, pigment, and dispersing agents are fed into a twin-screw extruder, where they are melted, mixed under high shear, and extruded through a die into strands. The strands are cooled (typically in a water bath) and cut into uniform pellets. The compounding process is where quality is built: proper screw design, temperature control, and shear energy determine how well the pigment is dispersed.

The quality of a masterbatch is determined during compounding, not during processing. A well-compounded masterbatch will disperse easily in any properly configured processing machine. A poorly compounded one will show streaks, specks, and color variation regardless of how the processor adjusts their parameters.

Types of masterbatch

Color masterbatch

The most common type. Color masterbatch contains one or more pigments in a carrier resin, formulated to produce a specific color at a defined let-down ratio. Standard colors follow RAL, Pantone, or custom specifications. AGAMA's color masterbatch range includes formulations for PE, PP, PS, and engineering resins across the full color spectrum.

Black masterbatch

Black masterbatch deserves its own category because carbon black, the pigment used, behaves differently from other pigments and is used in enormous volumes. Carbon black loading, particle size, and structure determine not just the depth of black but also UV protection, conductivity, and mechanical property effects. AGAMA's MB-110 Negro Kalo, MB-115 Negro Kalo, and MB-138 Negro Kalo are formulated for different applications and resin systems within the black masterbatch category.

White masterbatch

White masterbatch uses titanium dioxide (TiO2) as the primary pigment. TiO2 loading is typically high, 50% to 70%, because white requires significant pigment concentration for opacity. The quality of the TiO2 grade, the dispersion quality, and the carrier resin all affect the whiteness, opacity, and surface finish of the final part. AGAMA's MB-120 Blanco Shalom is a high-opacity white masterbatch designed for injection and extrusion applications.

Additive masterbatch

Additive masterbatch delivers functional properties rather than color. Common types include:

  • UV stabilizer masterbatch: Protects the plastic from degradation caused by ultraviolet radiation. Essential for outdoor applications like agricultural film, outdoor furniture, and automotive parts.
  • Slip and anti-block masterbatch: Reduces the coefficient of friction on film surfaces, preventing layers from sticking together. AGAMA's MB-105 Deslizante is formulated specifically for this function.
  • Antioxidant masterbatch: Prevents thermal and oxidative degradation during processing and in the finished product.
  • Anti-static masterbatch: Reduces static charge buildup on the surface of plastic parts, preventing dust attraction and handling problems.
  • Flame retardant masterbatch: Adds fire resistance to meet regulatory and application-specific flame ratings.

How masterbatch is used in production

Let-down ratio

The let-down ratio is the proportion of masterbatch to natural resin, expressed as a percentage by weight. A typical let-down ratio is 2% to 4%, meaning 2 to 4 kg of masterbatch per 100 kg of natural resin. The ratio depends on the pigment loading in the masterbatch and the color intensity required in the final part.

The supplier specifies the recommended let-down ratio for each masterbatch formulation. Using less than the recommended amount produces a weaker color; using more wastes material and can affect mechanical properties.

Dosing equipment

Masterbatch is dosed using a feeder mounted on the throat of the processing machine. There are two main types:

  • Volumetric feeders: Measure the masterbatch by volume using a screw or disc mechanism. They are less expensive but less accurate, especially when the masterbatch pellet size differs from the natural resin pellet size.
  • Gravimetric feeders: Measure by weight using a loss-in-weight system. They are more accurate and self-correcting, making them the preferred choice for applications where color consistency is critical.

Processing compatibility

Masterbatch works across all major plastics processing methods:

  • Injection molding: The most common application. Masterbatch pellets blend with natural resin in the hopper and are mixed during plasticization in the screw and barrel.
  • Extrusion: Used in film, sheet, pipe, and profile extrusion. The longer residence time in the extruder provides thorough mixing.
  • Blow molding: Used for bottles, containers, and industrial hollow parts. Color uniformity across the parison is critical and well-dispersed masterbatch achieves this reliably.

Advantages of masterbatch over raw pigment

There are compelling reasons why masterbatch has largely replaced the practice of adding raw pigment powder directly at the machine:

  • Better dispersion: Pre-compounding ensures the pigment is uniformly distributed, eliminating streaks and specks that occur when raw powder fails to disperse in the melt.
  • Cleanliness: Pellets produce no dust, keeping the production environment clean and eliminating the respiratory hazards associated with pigment powder handling.
  • Dosing accuracy: Pellets are easier to meter accurately than fine powders, improving batch-to-batch color consistency.
  • Reduced contamination: Color changes are faster and cleaner because there is no residual pigment dust in hoppers, feeders, or the production area.
  • Simplified inventory: A concentrated masterbatch takes up less storage space than the equivalent volume of raw pigment plus carrier resin purchased separately.

How to choose the right masterbatch

Selecting masterbatch is not just about picking a color. Here are the variables to define before placing an order:

  1. Base resin: The carrier resin in the masterbatch must be compatible with your base polymer. Specify the exact resin type and grade.
  2. Processing method: Injection, extrusion, and blow molding each have different shear and temperature profiles that affect how masterbatch disperses.
  3. Color target: Provide a physical color standard, Pantone or RAL reference, or a sample part. Verbal color descriptions are insufficient for precise matching.
  4. End-use requirements: Outdoor exposure (UV resistance), food contact (FDA compliance), temperature resistance, chemical resistance, and lightfastness all influence pigment selection.
  5. Let-down ratio preference: Higher-loaded masterbatch costs more per kilogram but is used in smaller amounts. The total cost per part may be lower with a higher-loaded formulation.
  6. Volume: Annual volume affects pricing, lead time, and whether a custom formulation or a standard catalog color makes more economic sense.
