USMCA Rules of Origin for Plastics: What US Buyers Need to Know

Sourcing masterbatch, pigments, or additives from Mexico can qualify for duty-free entry into the United States under the USMCA. But only if the product meets rules of origin. Here is a practical breakdown of what qualifies, how regional value content is calculated, and how to avoid the most common compliance mistakes.

Why rules of origin matter for plastics buyers

The United States-Mexico-Canada Agreement replaced NAFTA in 2020 and tightened the rules that determine whether a product qualifies for preferential tariff treatment. For plastics raw materials like masterbatch, pigments, and additives, the stakes are direct: goods that meet the rules enter the US at zero duty, while those that do not may face tariff rates of 3.1% to 6.5% depending on the HTS classification, plus potential anti-dumping or countervailing duties if the inputs originate in certain countries.

If you are a US manufacturer sourcing color or additive concentrates from Mexico, understanding these rules is not optional. It is the difference between a cost-competitive supply chain and an expensive one.

What qualifies as "originating" under USMCA

A product is considered originating in the USMCA territory when it meets one of three criteria:

  • Wholly obtained or produced: The product is entirely grown, harvested, or extracted in one or more USMCA countries. For plastics, this is rare since polymer resins often have global supply chains.
  • Produced entirely from originating materials: Every input in the product already qualifies as originating. If a Mexican masterbatch producer uses only USMCA-origin carrier resin and USMCA-origin pigment, the finished masterbatch qualifies automatically.
  • Tariff shift and/or regional value content (RVC): The product undergoes a specified change in tariff classification within a USMCA country, or meets a minimum RVC threshold. This is the most common path for masterbatch and pigment concentrates.

Tariff classification for masterbatch and pigments

Masterbatch typically falls under HTS Chapter 32 (pigment dispersions in plastics) or Chapter 39 (polymers containing colorants or additives), depending on formulation and concentration. The specific tariff shift rules vary by heading, but the general principle is consistent: if the manufacturing process in Mexico transforms the inputs enough to change the tariff heading, the finished product qualifies.

For a Mexican manufacturer like AGAMA that compounds carrier resin with pigment concentrates, the production process typically satisfies the tariff shift requirement. The raw pigment powder (Chapter 32) is dispersed into a polymer carrier (Chapter 39) to produce a finished masterbatch pellet, which constitutes a change in tariff classification.

Regional value content: the two calculation methods

When a tariff shift alone does not qualify the product, or when a product-specific rule requires it, the exporter must demonstrate that enough value was added within the USMCA region. There are two methods:

Transaction value method

RVC = ((Transaction Value - Value of Non-Originating Materials) / Transaction Value) x 100

The minimum threshold is typically 75% under this method for most plastics products. Transaction value is the price actually paid or payable for the good.

Net cost method

RVC = ((Net Cost - Value of Non-Originating Materials) / Net Cost) x 100

The minimum threshold is typically 65% under this method. Net cost excludes royalties, shipping, packing, and sales promotion costs, making it more conservative but sometimes more favorable when non-originating material costs are high relative to the sale price.

For most masterbatch and pigment products manufactured in Mexico with a mix of domestic and imported inputs, the transaction value method at 75% is the standard path. Mexican labor, energy, overhead, and domestic resin content usually push the RVC well above the threshold.

USMCA certification requirements

Unlike NAFTA, which required a specific government form (CBP Form 434), USMCA allows any format for the certificate of origin as long as it includes nine required data elements:

  1. Certifier identification (name, title, address, contact)
  2. Exporter identification (if different from certifier)
  3. Producer identification (if different from exporter)
  4. Importer identification
  5. Description of the good and HTS classification
  6. Origin criterion (which rule the product meets)
  7. Blanket period (if the certification covers multiple shipments)
  8. Authorized signature and date
  9. Declaration that the information is true and accurate

The certification can be issued by the producer, exporter, or importer. For US buyers sourcing from Mexico, the cleanest approach is to request the certification from the Mexican producer, since they hold the production records and material cost data needed to substantiate the claim.

Common mistakes US buyers make

After years of working under NAFTA rules, many buyers and their customs brokers carry assumptions that no longer apply. Here are the most frequent errors:

1. Assuming "Made in Mexico" equals USMCA-qualifying

A product manufactured in Mexico does not automatically qualify for duty-free treatment. If the non-originating material content is too high, or if the production process does not achieve the required tariff shift, the product may not meet rules of origin even though it was physically produced in Mexico.

2. Not requesting the certificate before shipment

The certificate of origin should be in hand before the goods arrive at the US border. Retroactive certification is possible but creates delays, broker fees, and the risk of paying duties upfront that must later be recovered through a refund claim.

3. Using outdated NAFTA certificates

NAFTA certificates are no longer valid. The data elements and format requirements changed under USMCA, and US Customs and Border Protection will reject NAFTA-format certificates for shipments after the transition date.

4. Ignoring de minimis rules

USMCA includes a de minimis provision: if non-originating materials account for no more than 10% of the transaction value of the good, the product may still qualify as originating even if it does not meet the tariff shift rule. Many buyers overlook this provision and assume their product does not qualify when it actually does.

5. Not verifying the supplier's record-keeping

Under USMCA, a certification can be verified by the importing country's customs authority. If the Mexican producer cannot substantiate the origin claim with production records and material sourcing documentation, the duty-free treatment can be revoked retroactively, with interest and penalties. Ask your supplier about their record retention practices before relying on their certification.

How to request a USMCA certificate from a Mexican supplier

If you are sourcing masterbatch, pigments, or additives from Mexico, here is the practical process:

  • Ask early: Include the USMCA certificate request in your purchase order or initial inquiry. Do not wait until the goods are ready to ship.
  • Specify blanket vs. single shipment: If you plan to order regularly, request a blanket certification covering up to 12 months of shipments for the same product.
  • Verify the nine data elements: Review the certificate against the USMCA requirements before forwarding it to your customs broker.
  • Keep records for five years: Both the importer and exporter must retain origin-related documentation for at least five years under USMCA.
