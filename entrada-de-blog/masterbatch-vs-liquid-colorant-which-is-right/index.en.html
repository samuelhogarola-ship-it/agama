Masterbatch vs. Liquid Colorant: Which Is Right for Your Plastics Process?

Choosing between masterbatch and liquid colorant is one of the most consequential decisions in plastics coloring. Each format has distinct advantages depending on your resin, process, and production volume. This guide breaks down the real differences so you can make the right call.

Two formats, one goal

Both masterbatch and liquid colorant exist to solve the same problem: getting consistent, uniform color into a plastic part. But they do it in fundamentally different ways, and those differences ripple through your entire production process, from dosing equipment to housekeeping to final part quality.

What is masterbatch?

Masterbatch is a concentrated mixture of pigments or additives dispersed in a carrier resin, delivered as solid pellets. The carrier resin is typically compatible with the base polymer being processed. During production, masterbatch pellets are blended with natural (uncolored) resin at a predetermined let-down ratio, usually between 1% and 5% by weight.

The pigment loading in masterbatch is high, typically 15% to 65% depending on the color and application. This concentrated format means you use a small amount of masterbatch relative to the total resin volume, and the mixing happens mechanically in the screw and barrel of the processing machine.

AGAMA produces a full range of color and additive masterbatch formulated for PE, PP, PS, and engineering resins.

What is liquid colorant?

Liquid colorant (also called liquid color or liquid dispersion) suspends pigments in a liquid carrier, typically a plasticizer, oil, or liquid polymer. It is dosed volumetrically using a pump system that injects the colorant into the throat of the processing machine, where it mixes with the natural resin during melting.

Liquid colorants are typically dosed at 0.5% to 3% by weight, with pigment loadings that can exceed those of masterbatch on a per-unit basis because the liquid carrier allows finer dispersion of certain pigment types.

Head-to-head comparison

Dosing precision

Liquid colorant has the edge here. Volumetric pump systems can dose liquid color with high accuracy, and adjustments are immediate. There is no lag between changing the dose setting and seeing the effect in the melt stream.

Masterbatch is dosed gravimetrically or volumetrically using a feeder mounted on the machine throat. Modern gravimetric feeders achieve excellent precision, but the response time to dose changes is slower because the pellet-to-melt transition introduces a buffer.

Cleanliness and changeovers

Masterbatch wins decisively. Pellets are dry, contained, and easy to handle. Changing colors means purging the barrel and swapping the masterbatch hopper, a process that takes minutes. Spills are swept up.

Liquid colorant is messier by nature. Pumps, lines, and injection nozzles must be flushed between color changes. Spills stain equipment and floors. In facilities running multiple colors across shifts, the cleanup time and waste generated by liquid systems can be significant.

In a multi-color shop running 6 or more color changes per week, the housekeeping overhead of liquid color can add up to several hours of lost production time versus masterbatch.

Color consistency

Both formats can deliver excellent batch-to-batch color consistency when the dosing system is properly calibrated and the colorant is well-formulated. However, liquid colorant can achieve marginally better dispersion of certain difficult pigments (particularly organic pigments and carbon black) because the liquid carrier pre-wets the pigment particles before they enter the melt.

Masterbatch achieves excellent dispersion through the compounding process itself. A well-made masterbatch from a quality supplier will deliver consistent color across batches without requiring adjustments on the production floor.

Cost

Masterbatch generally has a lower per-kilogram cost for standard colors and applications. There is no capital investment required beyond a simple feeder, which most processing shops already own.

Liquid colorant requires a pump dosing system, which represents a capital investment of several thousand dollars per machine. The liquid colorant itself can be cost-competitive on a per-part basis for high-volume runs, but the system investment, maintenance, and changeover costs tilt the total cost of ownership higher for most operations.

Storage and shelf life

Masterbatch pellets are stable, non-hygroscopic in most formulations, and have a shelf life measured in years when stored properly. They do not separate, settle, or degrade at ambient temperatures.

Liquid colorant can settle or separate over time and may require agitation before use. Temperature extremes can affect viscosity and pumpability. Shelf life is typically 6 to 12 months, and partially used containers need to be sealed to prevent contamination or skinning.

Resin compatibility

Masterbatch uses a carrier resin that must be compatible with the base polymer. This is straightforward for commodity resins like PE and PP, but can become complex for engineering resins or multi-layer applications where the carrier resin could affect mechanical properties or adhesion.

Liquid colorant is sometimes positioned as "universal" because the liquid carrier does not introduce a second polymer into the system. In practice, the liquid carrier itself can affect properties like clarity, surface finish, and weld line strength, so compatibility still needs to be verified.

Which process suits which format?

Injection molding

Masterbatch is the dominant format in injection molding. The process already uses pelletized resin, so adding masterbatch pellets through a feeder integrates seamlessly. Color changes are frequent in many injection molding shops, and the clean changeover advantage of masterbatch is decisive.

Extrusion (film, sheet, profile)

Both formats work well in extrusion. Masterbatch is more common for pipe, profile, and sheet extrusion. Liquid colorant finds a niche in blown film where ultra-thin gauge requires the finest possible pigment dispersion and where dedicated lines run a single color for extended periods, minimizing changeover concerns.

Blow molding

Masterbatch is standard for blow molding. The process demands excellent color uniformity across the parison, and masterbatch achieves this reliably. Liquid colorant is occasionally used for very large blow molded parts where the extended cycle time allows for thorough mixing.

What about raw pigment powder?

Some processors still color resin by adding dry pigment powder directly at the machine. This is the lowest-cost approach on a raw material basis, but it creates significant problems: poor dispersion leading to color streaks, pigment dust contamination in the facility, inconsistent dosing, and health and safety concerns from airborne pigment particles.

For all but the most cost-constrained applications, either masterbatch or liquid colorant is a better choice than raw pigment powder. The pre-dispersion in both formats eliminates the dust, improves color consistency, and simplifies the production process.

Making the decision

For most plastics processors, masterbatch is the right default choice. It is cleaner, simpler, requires less capital investment, and works well across all major processing methods. Liquid colorant earns its place in specific scenarios: high-volume dedicated lines, applications requiring exceptional pigment dispersion, or situations where the liquid format provides a measurable quality or cost advantage that justifies the system investment.
