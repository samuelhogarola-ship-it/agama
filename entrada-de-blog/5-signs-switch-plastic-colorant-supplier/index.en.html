5 Signs It's Time to Switch Your Plastic Colorant Supplier

Switching suppliers is disruptive. Qualifying a new masterbatch or pigment source takes time, testing, and internal coordination. But staying with the wrong supplier costs more. Here are five warning signs that the relationship is no longer working.

The cost of loyalty to the wrong supplier

Most plastics processors stick with their colorant supplier for years, sometimes decades. There is good reason for that: qualifying a new color source means running trials, adjusting process parameters, and getting customer approval on color standards. Nobody wants to do that work unless they have to.

But there is a threshold where the cost of staying exceeds the cost of switching. When your supplier becomes a source of production problems rather than solutions, the math changes. The five signs below are the ones that experienced processors recognize too late.

Sign 1: Color inconsistency between batches

This is the most damaging problem a colorant supplier can create. You order the same masterbatch or pigment formulation, receive two different lots, and they do not match. The color shift may be subtle under plant lighting but obvious under daylight or when parts from different production runs are placed side by side.

Batch-to-batch color variation has real consequences:

  • Customer complaints and returns
  • Production stoppages while operators adjust let-down ratios to compensate
  • Scrapped parts that fall outside the color tolerance window
  • Time spent on incoming inspection that should not be necessary

A quality colorant supplier controls color consistency through spectrophotometric measurement, standardized raw material sourcing, and documented compounding processes. If your supplier cannot hold a Delta E of 1.0 or better between batches on standard colors, the problem is systemic, not occasional.

If you are adjusting your process to compensate for your supplier's color variation, you are paying for their quality problem with your production efficiency.

Sign 2: Lead times keep growing

A masterbatch order that used to ship in 5 business days now takes 10. Then 15. The supplier blames raw material shortages, production backlogs, or logistics issues. Occasionally, every supplier faces disruptions. But when lead times trend upward consistently, the underlying cause is usually one of three things:

  • Capacity constraints: The supplier has taken on more business than their production can handle.
  • Cash flow problems: They are delaying raw material purchases until customer payments come in, creating a just-in-time chain that breaks at the slightest disruption.
  • Poor planning: They lack the inventory management and production scheduling systems to deliver reliably.

None of these are problems you can solve from the customer side. If your supplier's lead times have grown by 50% or more over the past year without a clear, temporary cause, start qualifying an alternative.

Sign 3: No technical support when issues arise

You are running a new resin and the existing masterbatch is not dispersing properly. Or you have changed a processing parameter and the color is shifting. You need a technical conversation with someone who understands pigment chemistry, carrier resin compatibility, and processing conditions.

If your supplier's response is silence, a voicemail that is never returned, or a sales rep who cannot answer technical questions, you have a transactional supplier, not a technical partner. The distinction matters because color and additive issues are inherently technical. A supplier who can only take orders and ship product is fine when everything works. The moment something goes wrong, you are on your own.

What to look for in a replacement:

  • A dedicated technical contact who understands resin behavior and pigment dispersion
  • Willingness to visit your production floor or conduct virtual troubleshooting sessions
  • Access to lab facilities for color matching, testing, and reformulation
  • Same-day or next-day response to technical inquiries

Sign 4: Cannot get samples or custom colors

You have a customer requesting a specific Pantone match or a custom color for a new product line. You ask your supplier for a sample, and the response is either a flat refusal, a multi-week timeline, or a minimum order quantity that makes sampling impractical.

The ability to produce samples and develop custom colors is a fundamental capability for any serious masterbatch or pigment supplier. If your supplier only stocks a fixed catalog of standard colors and cannot or will not develop custom formulations, they are limiting your ability to serve your own customers.

A capable supplier should be able to:

  • Produce a color match sample within 3 to 5 business days from a physical reference or Pantone/RAL specification
  • Provide trial quantities (5 to 25 kg) for production testing before committing to a full order
  • Iterate on the color match based on your feedback from actual production conditions
  • Maintain the custom formulation for repeat orders with documented specifications

Sign 5: Cannot provide compliance documentation

Depending on your end market, you may need your colorant supplier to provide documentation proving that their products comply with specific regulations: FDA food contact, RoHS, REACH, California Proposition 65, or industry-specific standards like NSF for water contact applications.

If your supplier cannot produce these documents, or if the documents they provide are outdated, vague, or clearly generic rather than product-specific, you have a compliance risk that extends to your own products and your customers.

The regulatory landscape for plastics colorants is tightening, not loosening. Heavy metal content limits, migration testing requirements, and substance restrictions are becoming more demanding across industries. A supplier who treats compliance documentation as an afterthought is one who may expose your company to product recalls, customer claims, or regulatory penalties.

For US buyers sourcing from Mexico, trade compliance adds another layer. Your supplier should be able to issue USMCA certificates of origin for duty-free treatment, with proper documentation and record-keeping to withstand a customs audit.

How to transition without disrupting production

Switching suppliers does not have to be a cliff edge. Here is the sequence that minimizes risk:

  1. Identify your top 3 to 5 colors by volume. These are the formulations that matter most. Start the qualification process with these.
  2. Request samples from the new supplier. Provide physical color standards, resin specifications, and processing conditions.
  3. Run production trials. Test the new masterbatch or pigment under actual production conditions, not just lab conditions. Evaluate color, dispersion, processability, and any impact on mechanical properties.
  4. Run parallel supply for 2 to 3 months. Keep ordering from your current supplier while building confidence in the new one. This eliminates the risk of a gap if the new qualification takes longer than expected.
  5. Transition remaining colors. Once the high-volume formulations are qualified, work through the rest of your color palette at a manageable pace.
